## What causes low water pressure?

Nine times out of ten, it's one of these:

**Mineral buildup.** If you live in a hard water area — and most of us do — minerals settle inside your pipes and fixtures over the years. Showerheads are the usual victim. You pull the head off and there's a crusty white gunk blocking the holes. That's calcium. Vinegar gets rid of it.

**Clogged pipes.** Less common but more serious. Old galvanised pipes rust from the inside out. The rust builds up and narrows the pipe until you get a trickle instead of a flow. If your house was built before the 1970s and hasn't had the pipes replaced, this is worth checking.

**A leak somewhere.** If you've got low pressure and an unexplained damp patch somewhere, or your water bill just went up for no reason, you might have a leak. Small leaks steal pressure. Big leaks steal money.

**The pressure regulator's gone wrong.** Your house has a pressure regulator — a valve that keeps the pressure consistent. If it's worn out, pressure drops. You'll notice it across every tap and the shower. It's a relatively cheap fix, but you need a plumber to do it.

**The municipal supply has issues.** Sometimes it's not you. A burst main down the street, a pump station going down, high demand on a hot day. If your neighbours are also getting a trickle, call the water authority before calling a plumber.

## What you can fix yourself

If it's the showerhead or a tap, yeah — you can handle that. Takes 20 minutes.

Pull the head off. Soak it in vinegar for an hour. Scrub the holes with an old toothbrush. Put it back. If the pressure comes back, you just saved yourself a call-out fee.

Same for the aerator on a tap. Unscrew it, soak it, scrub it, screw it back. Done.

If it's the whole house and it's gradual — low pressure that's been getting worse over months — it's probably the pipes or the regulator. Not a DIY job.

If it's sudden — one day you had normal pressure, the next day you didn't — check for leaks first. If there's no leak, call someone.

## When to call a plumber

Call a plumber if:

- You've tried the easy stuff (showerhead, aerators) and the pressure's still low
- The pressure's low everywhere, not just one tap
- You think you've got a leak
- The pressure dropped suddenly and you don't know why
- Your pipes are old and you're wondering if they need replacing
- You've got low hot water pressure but normal cold — that's usually the water heater, not the pipes

A decent plumber will tell you what's wrong and what it'll cost before they start. If they won't give you a number, find another plumber.

## What a plumber actually does

First they figure out where the problem is. Whole house or one fixture? Hot water or both? Gradual or sudden? That tells them what to look at.

Then they test the pressure at different points — the main line, after the regulator, at a few fixtures. That tells them if the problem's in the street, in your house, or in a specific fixture.

Then they fix what's broken. Could be cleaning a fixture. Could be replacing a regulator. Could be rerouting a section of pipe. Depends on what they find.

## How much does it cost?

Depends on what's wrong.

Cleaning a showerhead or tap aerator: $50-$100. Quick job, minimal parts.

Replacing a pressure regulator: $200-$400. The part's cheap. The labour's the cost, because it involves shutting off the water, cutting into the line, and testing.

Pipe work: $500-$3,000+ depending on how much needs doing. If you need a section of pipe replaced, that's a few hours of labour plus parts. If you need a full repipe, that's thousands.

Water heater service: $150-$300. Flushing the tank, checking the elements, replacing a valve.

A plumbing inspection: $100-$200. Worth it if you don't know what's going on and you want someone to tell you before you commit to a repair.

These are ballpark figures. A good plumber will give you a real number after they've looked at it. Not a "it depends" — a real number.

## The short version

Low water pressure has a cause. Most causes are fixable. Some are $50 DIY jobs. Some are $500 plumbing jobs.

If it's one fixture, try cleaning it first. If it's the whole house, or if you've already cleaned the fixtures and it's still low, call a plumber.

And if you're not sure — call a plumber anyway. A proper diagnosis is cheaper than guessing wrong and doing the wrong thing.
